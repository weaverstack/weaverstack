Fabric Engineering Guides

Organising Microsoft Fabric workspaces for enterprise data sharing

Microsoft Fabric makes it easy for teams across your organisation to share data. As more teams build on one another's work, you also need to see how that work connects: what a failure affects, and what needs to run once it is fixed. This guide shows how to organise Fabric workspaces so each team keeps its independence, tested across 500 workspaces.

Why organisations share data in Microsoft Fabric

When your organisation chooses Microsoft Fabric, one reason is often its enterprise sharing. OneLake gives the whole tenant one data lake, shortcuts let one team read another team's tables without copying them, and workspaces give each team a place of its own. A central data team can publish trusted data once, and business teams can build on it directly instead of maintaining their own copies.

That openness creates a new problem. Once dozens or hundreds of teams read one another's data, a failure in one workspace can affect tables and reports in many others, and no single team's job history shows how far it reached.

1. The problem: shared data, separate teams

Consider the following setup, which is common when an organisation adopts Fabric for self-service data:

  • A central team owns the Bronze Lakehouse in one workspace.
  • Another team owns the Silver Lakehouse and Gold Warehouse in a second.
  • Shared semantic models live in a third.
  • Dozens or hundreds of business areas own workspaces of their own. They read central data, and sometimes one another's.

Now suppose the central customer load fails overnight, and you run Finance's Warehouse. The questions arrive at once:

  • Which tables and reports across the organisation are affected?
  • Are any of Finance's tables built on the failed data?
  • Once the central team repairs it, what should Finance reload, and when?

A central scheduler can coordinate the loads, or each team can run on its own schedule. Either way, Finance cannot answer these questions from its own job history, because the cause sits in another team's workspace.

The answer is not to merge every team into one project. Keep each team's ownership, and connect the records each team already keeps: what each table reads, and what happened when it last loaded.

2. What Fabric already gives you

Fabric already provides two of the foundations: every item and object can be named precisely, and OneLake connects data across workspaces. Your data can stay with the team that owns it.

2.1 Every object has a full name

When someone says CustomerSales failed, you need to know which team's table they mean. For a Warehouse table, the workspace, item type, item name, schema and object name identify it exactly. A Folder uses a path instead of a schema; a semantic model or Report is an item in its own right.

2.2 OneLake shares data across workspaces

Every Fabric tenant has a single OneLake (Microsoft 2026a). Its shortcuts let an item in one workspace read data held by an item in another, including a Lakehouse reading a Warehouse, without copying it (Microsoft 2026b).

Spark can address tables in other workspaces directly by four-part name. Warehouse T-SQL reaches only items in its own workspace, so each business team in this test brought the central Gold tables into its workspace through shortcuts in a small bridge Lakehouse, and read them through that Lakehouse's SQL analytics endpoint.

Fabric also offers item lineage and impact analysis across workspaces. Recovering from a failure needs one level more: how the tables and loads themselves depend on one another, which loads need attention, and what should run next.

3. The two missing pieces

To answer Finance's questions, you need two records connected across every team:

3.1 Which tables read which

Finance's shortcut already names the central item and path it reads. Bring both teams' catalogues together, and Weaver matches that source to the central team's table. Nobody maintains a separate cross-team map, and it never drifts out of date.

3.2 What happened when each last loaded

A successful Finance load can still be behind if the central customer table has been repaired since it ran. Compare Finance's load record with its source, and you can tell a failed step from work that simply needs catching up. The same comparison traces a central failure through every table and model that depends on it.

Each team keeps its own catalogue. You choose which catalogues to include, from a pair of teams to the whole organisation, and Weaver connects their dependencies for that investigation or run.

3.3 A bonus: running teams' work together

The same connections let independent teams' work run side by side. Each reader waits only for the sources it needs, including a SQL endpoint refresh or OneLake publication between engines, while unrelated work carries on. The performance guide explains how that scheduling works.

4. Testing it across 500 workspaces

To show this working at enterprise scale, the test used 500 Fabric workspaces on an FTL64 trial capacity in Australia East. Each represented an independently owned project with its own catalogue and configuration. The teams and their tables were synthetic and small, so the test measures coordination across workspaces rather than data volume.

After running every team together, the test broke the central Bronze table BRZ.Customer, then followed the organisation through finding the impact, repairing the source and recovering each team's work.

5. How Weaver connects the teams

With Weaver, each team keeps these records in a catalogue in its own Fabric Warehouse. Build records the objects and their dependencies; Load and Test record what ran. When you need the wider view, you bring the relevant catalogues together. Every team's project stays where it is.

5.1 The Weaver CLI

After the central repair, Team 05 catches up with one command. From its own project, it names its own work and includes the central catalogues that explain its upstream state:

Reload Team 05's stale work

$ weaver load SPREAD_TEAM_05 --workspace SPREAD_TEAM_05 --stale \
  --catalogue SPREAD_TEAM_05/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_BRONZE/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_CENTRAL/Warehouse/Catalogue

The central catalogues provide context; only Team 05's work runs. Select several teams when you want a coordinated run instead. Weaver orders the work and records each result in its owner's catalogue. The across-catalogue guide covers the setup, selectors and permissions.

5.2 The Catalogue Dashboard

If you would rather explore in Power BI, open the Catalogue Dashboard. Select Team 05 on the Workspaces page, follow the teams that supply it, then use Health and Orchestration to investigate the failed load. Every page narrows to your workspace, item or object.

The snapshot below shows the Dashboard over ten catalogues. Direct Lake reads up to ten; for a larger estate, deploy the Dashboard in Import mode and refresh it to read new records.

Readiness steps are SQL endpoint refreshes and OneLake publication waits that let readers see new data. Health times are for a full check of the 500-workspace estate with every target inventory enabled. For data-volume results, see the performance guide.

6.1 The full reach of a failure, in minutes

From one failed Bronze load, Health traced 601 Warehouse tables, 50 Lakehouse tables and 101 semantic models in 450 downstream workspaces. Each object was counted once, and each was traced to its cause. An affected object had not necessarily failed: it could still hold its last good result, which is exactly what its owners need to know.

The 49 thin-report workspaces read the central semantic model, so their reports recover as soon as that model does.

6.2 Each team recovers on its own schedule

The central team repaired and reloaded its own work first. Team 05 then saw that its upstream data was fixed and reloaded just its two tables, without waiting for the other 496 teams. The central catalogues told Team 05 what was ready; they did not pull other teams' work into its run.

6.3 Teams that read one another's data

Teams 01 and 02 read each other's summaries. Weaver ran the cycle in a consistent order and reported it in Health, so the one remaining amber finding pointed straight at it. The cycle guide explains how cycles run and how Health assesses them.

6.4 Coordinating when you need to

Everyday work stays with each team. When a wider recovery is needed, select the teams together and add --stale: Weaver loads only the work whose recorded state needs it, in dependency order. In this test that recovered 746 object loads across 447 workspaces in one run.

6.5 Checking 500 catalogues in minutes

A full Health check read every one of the 500 catalogues, inventoried their physical targets and assessed the joined estate from a desktop in five to seven minutes.

7. Share data across Fabric without losing track of it

Fabric's sharing is a reason to choose it. Connect the dependency and load records, and you keep that benefit as the number of teams grows: each team keeps its project, catalogue and schedule, and the organisation can still see and recover across all of them.

  1. If you own a shared source:See every team that depends on it, and coordinate the recovery.
  2. If you consume one:See what held up your work, and catch it up when you are ready.

The same records serve both a single team and the whole organisation. You coordinate when it helps, without turning independent teams into one shared deployment.
