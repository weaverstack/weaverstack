Fabric Engineering Guides

Organising Fabric workspaces for independent teams

Microsoft Fabric allows each business unit to own its workspaces, projects and schedules. Operating those workspaces as one estate also requires a record of the data dependencies between them. This guide reports an experiment across 500 Fabric workspaces in which a shared-source failure was traced to its downstream consumers, one team recovered independently, and the limits of the wider campaign were recorded.

Introduction

A Microsoft Fabric workspace design is also a decision about data ownership: which team owns each dataset, who may change it, and how teams depend on one another. Assigning a workspace to each team establishes clear ownership. The design must also account for the dependencies that form once those teams share data.

Consider a Finance team that owns a Warehouse and its load schedule. Its sales analysis reads a customer table maintained by a central data team. When the central customer load fails, Finance must determine which of its tables are affected and, once the central team has repaired the source, which of them must be reloaded.

These questions arise at two levels. The central team must establish the extent of the failure across the organisation; Finance must restore its own work on its own schedule. Separate projects should clarify ownership without requiring the dependencies between them to be reconstructed during an incident.

1. Self-service data and cross-team coordination

Organisations that adopt Fabric for self-service data commonly arrive at a structure similar to the following:

  • A central team owns the Bronze Lakehouse in one workspace.
  • Another team owns the Silver Lakehouse and Gold Warehouse in a second.
  • Shared semantic models are held in a third.
  • Business units, potentially several hundred, own workspaces of their own. They read central data and, in some cases, one another's.

Loads may be coordinated by a central scheduler or run on each team's own schedule. In either model, Finance must be able to confirm whether its most recent successful load used current central data. Finance's job history alone cannot establish that.

The approach examined here retains each team's ownership and connects the dependency and load records that each team already maintains. The organisation can then investigate one team's work or coordinate a wider recovery without routing every change through a shared project.

2. Existing Fabric capabilities

Two existing Fabric capabilities support this approach: items and objects that can be identified unambiguously, and OneLake connections between workspaces. Data remains in the workspace of the team that owns it.

2.1 Fully qualified object names

A report that CustomerSales has failed is ambiguous until it identifies the owning team. For a Warehouse table, the workspace, item type, item name, schema and object name together identify it. A Folder is identified by a path rather than a schema; a semantic model or Report is an item in its own right.

Finance's shortcut already identifies the central source it reads. When the participating catalogues are supplied together, Weaver matches that source to the central team's recorded installation, so no separate cross-team mapping needs to be maintained.

2.2 OneLake shares data across workspaces

Every Fabric tenant has a single OneLake instance (Microsoft 2026a). OneLake shortcuts allow an item in one workspace to read data held by an item in another, including a Lakehouse reading a Warehouse, without copying it (Microsoft 2026b).

In this experiment, Spark addressed tables in other workspaces directly by four-part name. Warehouse T-SQL can reach only items within its own workspace, so each business team exposed the central Gold tables in its workspace through shortcuts in a small bridge Lakehouse and queried them through that Lakehouse's SQL analytics endpoint.

Fabric also provides item lineage and impact analysis, including downstream items in other workspaces. This guide addresses a narrower operational question: how the managed tables and loads depend on one another, which recorded operations require attention, and what should run next.

3. Two required records

Answering Finance's recovery question requires two records: what each table reads, and the outcome of its most recent load.

3.1 Recorded dependencies

Finance's shortcut identifies the central item and path it reads. With both catalogues supplied, Weaver matches that source to the central team's installed table and links the recorded dependencies. Teams do not maintain the same relationship a second time.

3.2 Load outcomes

A successful Finance load can still be out of date if the central customer table has been repaired since it ran. Comparing Finance's load record with the state of its source distinguishes a failed step from work that only needs to catch up, and allows a central failure to be traced through the tables and models that depend on it.

Each team retains its own catalogue. The operator selects the catalogues to include, and Weaver connects their recorded dependencies for that investigation or run. The scope can range from two teams to every participating catalogue.

3.3 Concurrent execution

The same dependency records allow independent teams' work to run concurrently. Each reader waits only for the sources it requires, including any SQL endpoint refresh or OneLake publication between engines, while unrelated work proceeds. The performance guide describes this scheduling within a single project.

4. Experimental setup

The experiment used 500 Fabric workspaces on an FTL64 trial capacity in Australia East. Each workspace represented an independently owned project with its own catalogue and configuration. The teams and their small tables were synthetic: the experiment measured dependency tracking and execution across workspaces, not data-volume throughput.

After a successful coordinated baseline, a failure was introduced into the central Bronze table BRZ.Customer. A subsequent full coordinated Load failed one semantic-model refresh, so the campaign as a whole did not qualify end to end.

5. Implementation with Weaver

With Weaver, each team holds these records in a catalogue in its own Fabric Warehouse. Build records objects and their dependencies; Load and Test record what ran. Related catalogues are read together for an investigation or run, and each team's project remains where it is.

5.1 The Weaver CLI

After the central repair, Team 05 brings its work up to date with a single command, run from its configured project. The command selects Team 05's own work and supplies the central catalogues that describe its upstream state:

Reload Team 05's stale work

$ weaver load SPREAD_TEAM_05 --workspace SPREAD_TEAM_05 --stale \
  --catalogue SPREAD_TEAM_05/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_BRONZE/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_CENTRAL/Warehouse/Catalogue

The central catalogues provide context; only Team 05's work is selected to run. Selecting several teams produces a coordinated run, which Weaver orders and records in each owner's catalogue. The across-catalogue guide describes the setup, selectors and required permissions.

5.2 The Catalogue Dashboard

The same relationships can be examined in Power BI through the Catalogue Dashboard. Selecting Team 05 on the Workspaces page shows the teams that supply it; the Health and Orchestration pages show the failed load. Each page can be filtered to a workspace, item or object.

The snapshot below shows the interface over ten catalogues from an earlier run; it does not represent the 500-workspace result. For a larger estate, deploy the Dashboard in Import mode and refresh it to read new records; Direct Lake supports up to ten catalogues.

6.1 Impact of a shared-source failure

The impact assessment traced the failed Bronze load to 601 Warehouse tables, 50 Lakehouse tables and 101 semantic models in 450 downstream workspaces. The count excludes the failed source and counts each managed object once, regardless of the number of findings against it. An affected object had not necessarily failed; it could still hold the result of an earlier successful load.

The 49 thin-report workspaces read the central semantic model. Their data is restored when that model is, and they are not counted as separate managed table or model loads.

6.2 Independent recovery by each team

After the repair was built, the central team selected its own work: six object loads and four readiness steps completed across the three central catalogues. Team 05 then confirmed that the upstream data for its two Mart tables had been repaired and loaded its own stale work. Both object loads succeeded, and its subsequent catalogue-only Health report was green.

The upstream catalogues supplied context and readiness requirements but did not extend Team 05's selection to other teams' objects. Recovery of the remaining stale work was a separate operation with its own result.

6.3 Mutual dependencies between teams

Teams 01 and 02 read each other's summaries. The cycle was reported in both the baseline and the post-recovery Health assessments. After recovery, one amber stale-ancestor finding remained on that cycle, and the assessment reported it rather than reporting every catalogue as green. The cycle guide describes the execution and assessment rules.

6.4 Coordinated runs and recorded failures

The initial all-catalogue baseline and the subsequent selected stale recovery both succeeded. A further full coordinated Load did not: Team 496's semantic-model refresh failed after 1,159 other steps had succeeded. The campaign therefore has no successful final coordinated Load and no Health check following it.

A later rerun on an intermediate Health build also stopped during its impact assessment, before recovery began. The desktop process exhausted its file descriptors, and 388 of the 500 catalogues were reported unreadable. The Health implementation described in section 6.5 addresses this limit by reusing one set of HTTP connections for the Session. Neither attempt constitutes a qualified end-to-end 500-workspace campaign, and the successful individual stages and read-only checks do not offset those failures.

6.5 Health performance in Weaver 0.11.1

Weaver 0.11.1 reduces the time Health takes across many catalogues. Its pre-release build assessed the same 500-workspace estate from a desktop with all default target inventories enabled. Two checks, each from a newly started client, took approximately seven and five minutes, including Session close and native-driver cleanup. The first exceeded the six-minute target; the second met it. These are observed timings and do not guarantee that every check will complete within six minutes.

Each check covered discovery, catalogue reads, physical target inventories and the joined assessment. A complete check is one in which every selected catalogue was read and assessed; it does not imply that every catalogue was green. The checks ran after the interrupted rerun described above, which had reloaded only the central Bronze table. Fifty catalogues were therefore green and the remaining 450 correctly reported amber, each with a stale ancestor. This reflects the state of the estate at the time, not a regression from the 499 green catalogues reported earlier. These are Health measurements, not new Load or recovery results.

7. Conclusion: team ownership with coordinated operation

Teams can retain control of their Fabric work while the organisation retains the ability to operate across it. Each team keeps its project, catalogue and schedule; the recorded dependencies provide the wider view when a shared failure or a coordinated run requires one.

  1. Owners of a shared source:Identify the dependent teams and coordinate the selected recovery.
  2. Consumers of that source:Establish what delayed their work and bring it up to date on their own schedule.

The same records support both a single team's decision and an organisation-wide run. Coordination is available when it is required, without consolidating independent teams into a single shared deployment.
