Fabric Engineering Guides

Organising Fabric workspaces for independent teams

Teams can own separate Fabric projects and still coordinate the work between them. In this 50-workspace experiment, teams recovered their own work and joined a coordinated load using the dependencies recorded in their catalogues.

Team ownership and shared dependencies

Finance might run its own Warehouse and schedule, but read a customer table maintained centrally. When that table fails, Finance needs to understand which of its tables depend on it, and the central team needs to coordinate the repair.

1. Self-service data and the coordination problem

A common way to organise Microsoft Fabric workspaces for self-service data looks like this:

  • A central team owns the Bronze Lakehouse in one workspace.
  • Another team owns the Silver Lakehouse and Gold Warehouse in a second.
  • Shared semantic models live in a third.
  • Forty or fifty business areas own workspaces of their own. They read central data and occasionally one another's.

You can coordinate loads through a central scheduler or let teams manage their own schedules. In either case, someone still needs to understand the dependencies between teams and decide what to rerun after a failure. To decide whether a table is up to date, a team needs its latest load result and the state of the sources it reads.

An enterprise needs the advantages of both. Each team should be able to operate on its own, while the organisation can still see its dependencies, assess the impact of a failure and coordinate recovery when necessary.

2. What Fabric already provides

Two properties of Fabric make an organisation-wide dependency graph possible without moving any data or adding a central service.

2.1 Qualify the object you mean

A name such as CustomerSales needs context. In this example, the workspace, item type, item name, schema and object name tell a reader which Warehouse table is being discussed. Folders use paths rather than Warehouse schemas; a model or Report is an item in its own right.

The workspace, item and object name give readers the context they need to identify the table. When a shortcut reads another team's table, Weaver matches its source against the installations in the catalogues you supply and connects the dependency.

2.2 OneLake shares data across workspaces

Every Fabric tenant comes with a single OneLake instance (Microsoft 2026a). Its shortcuts let an item in one workspace read data held by an item in another, including a Lakehouse reading a Warehouse, without copying it (Microsoft 2026b).

In this experiment, Spark addressed tables in other workspaces directly by four-part name. A Warehouse's T-SQL reached only items in its own workspace, so each business team brought central Gold tables into its workspace through shortcuts in a small bridge Lakehouse and read them through that Lakehouse's SQL analytics endpoint.

Fabric also supplies item lineage and impact analysis, including downstream items in other workspaces. The question here is more specific: how do the managed tables and loads depend on one another, which recorded operations need attention, and what should run next?

3. The two missing pieces

Beyond what Fabric provides, an organisation-wide dependency graph needs two pieces of information about each object.

3.1 Connect the recorded dependencies

Finance's shortcut already identifies the central item and path it reads. Given both catalogues, Weaver matches that source to the central team's installed table and connects the recorded dependencies. The teams do not need to maintain the same connection again in a second map.

3.2 Follow the load results

Each object must also record its operational state: when it last loaded, whether that load succeeded, and when its upstream last changed. With that, a failed table can be traced to every object that read it, and an object that loaded successfully before its upstream was repaired can be identified as behind.

Each team keeps its own catalogue. Choose the catalogues to include, and Weaver connects their recorded dependencies for the investigation or run. You can inspect a pair of teams or bring the participating catalogues together for a wider view.

3.3 A bonus: concurrency

Once the dependencies are known, Weaver can schedule independent branches in different workspaces together. Each reader waits for its own upstream, including the SQL analytics endpoint refresh or OneLake publication between them. The techniques are the same as within one estate, described in Performance Engineering on Microsoft Fabric.

5. The implementation: Weaver

Weaver is an open-source data engineering framework for Microsoft Fabric. In this setup, each project keeps a catalogue in a Fabric Warehouse. Build records the managed objects and dependencies; Load and Test record their results. Those records let a caller investigate connected work without moving the teams into one project.

5.1 The Weaver CLI

Use --catalogue to choose the records Weaver reads. For Load and Test, use item or workspace selectors to choose the work it runs. With no item selector, Load and Test run the working workspace's catalogue by default.

Terminal: three participating catalogues
# Assess these catalogues and their recorded connections
$ weaver health --workspace SPREAD_TEAM_05 \
  --catalogue SPREAD_TEAM_05/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_BRONZE/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_CENTRAL/Warehouse/Catalogue

# Team 05 reloads only what is behind, reading its upstream for context
$ weaver load SPREAD_TEAM_05 --workspace SPREAD_TEAM_05 --stale \
  --catalogue SPREAD_TEAM_05/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_BRONZE/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_CENTRAL/Warehouse/Catalogue

Team 05 uses the central catalogues to understand its dependencies while loading its own tables. A run can also select work across several teams. Weaver orders that work together, records results in each team's catalogue and uses one workflow ID to connect the records. See working across catalogues for selection and assessment details.

Give the running identity read access to each catalogue you include. To load another team's objects, it also needs permission to run that work and record the results there. See the permissions reference for the command requirements.

5.2 The Catalogue Dashboard

Use the Catalogue Dashboard to explore the participating catalogues in Power BI. Start with Workspaces to see the connections, then use Health and Orchestration to investigate a failure. Filters narrow the pages by workspace, item and object.

The Dashboard reads up to ten catalogues over Direct Lake; use Import mode for larger sets and refresh it to pick up new records. Exported HTML is a snapshot of the deployed model's data, and a downloaded local copy opens without a running Fabric capacity.

6. Results

The experiment introduced a failure into BRZ.Customer and followed the recovery from three perspectives: the central team repairing the source, a business team updating its own tables, and an operator coordinating the remaining work across workspaces.

The coordinated run's end-to-end time includes catalogue reads, planning, Spark startup, execution and recording. The test used small tables on the FTL64 trial capacity to measure coordination across workspaces. For data-volume results, see the performance guide.

6.1 Knowing the impact of a failure

Health linked the failed Bronze load to 61 Warehouse tables, five Lakehouse tables and 11 semantic models across 45 downstream workspaces. The central team could see where the failure reached, and each business team could identify which of its own objects needed attention. Both were working from the same recorded cause.

The four thin-report workspaces read the central semantic model, so their data recovery follows that model's upstream recovery.

6.2 Each team recovers on its own terms

The central team reloaded six objects across Bronze, Silver/Gold and the shared semantic model. Weaver ordered the work and waited for the publications and endpoint refreshes its readers needed. Business teams retained control of when to reload their own tables.

After the central repair, Team 05 checked its two Mart tables, reloaded them and returned to green. Its project and schedule remained its own, and it could recover its work independently of the other business teams' runs.

6.3 Teams that read one another's data

Teams 01 and 02 read each other's summaries. Weaver reported that cycle and ran its members once in a consistent order. Where a dependency closed the loop, the consumer could use the result available from an earlier run. Health continued to report failures, pending work and overdue loads. The cycle guide explains the execution and assessment rules.

6.4 Coordination when the organisation needs it

For a wider recovery, a caller supplies the participating catalogues and selects the workspaces or items to rerun. --stale narrows that selection to work whose recorded state requires another load.

The invocation coordinates the selected work across projects. Teams can join that run without maintaining a permanent central scheduling service or a shared project.

7. Team ownership, coordinated operation

The experiment demonstrates how a central team and business teams can operate from the same dependency records while keeping separate projects and schedules.

  1. Central coordination.The central team can coordinate shared work across catalogues.
  2. Business-team ownership.Business teams can inspect the sources they depend on and recover their own work after an upstream repair.

Weaver records those relationships as teams build and run their projects. Bringing the catalogues together reuses that information, so teams can join coordinated operations while retaining ownership of their own work. They can do this without maintaining a second cross-team dependency map.
