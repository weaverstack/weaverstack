Fabric Engineering Guides

Organising Microsoft Fabric workspaces for enterprise data sharing

Self-service in Microsoft Fabric usually grows one team at a time. A central team publishes trusted data, and business teams build on it in their own workspaces and on their own schedules. This guide looks at what happens when a central load fails under that arrangement: what the central team needs to know, what each business team needs to know, and how both can find out without a central scheduler. It was tested across 50 workspaces and repeated at 500.

1. How self-service grows in Fabric

Consider how data sharing usually develops once an organisation adopts Fabric. A central team loads Bronze, Silver and Gold, and publishes trusted Gold tables for the rest of the organisation. Its responsibility ends there. It may not know who reads Gold, and it often has no access to their data: Finance, HR and Risk restrict their workspaces, as they should.

Business teams build on Gold through OneLake shortcuts instead of maintaining copies. Finance builds a Warehouse, Marketing a semantic model, and Risk a Lakehouse that also reads Finance's summary. Each team loads on its own schedule, usually set a little after the central team normally finishes. On most days, that is good enough.

Nobody designs the overall shape. It grows one shortcut at a time, and in time some teams read one another's data in both directions. This is what self-service is meant to produce. Teams own their data and their schedules, and the central team doesn't become a bottleneck.

2. When a central load fails

Now suppose the central customer load fails overnight. Finance's 6:15 job runs anyway and reports success, because Finance's own step worked. The two sides of the organisation now have different questions.

The central team needs to judge the impact:

  • Which teams' tables and reports are built on the failed data?
  • Who should be told once it is repaired?

Its view ends at Gold, though, and it shouldn't need access to other teams' data to find out.

Finance needs to know about its own work:

  • Are any of Finance's tables built on the failed data?
  • Once the central team repairs it, what should Finance reload, and when?

Finance can't answer either question from its own job history, because the cause sits in another team's workspace. Without a shared record, the usual options are to raise a ticket and wait, or to reload everything to be safe.

3. Why a central scheduler doesn't answer these questions

One response is to move every team's loads into a single orchestration, so that the central team's failure stops everything downstream of it. In a large organisation, that is rarely practical. Someone would have to model and maintain the dependencies of dozens of teams whose work changes every week. They would also need access to every team's workspace, and every team's schedule change would become a change to the central orchestration.

Leaving each team with its own scheduler doesn't answer the questions either. Fifty teams with 50 schedulers each know when their own jobs ran, but none of them can see what happened in the central team's schedule, or whether its sources were ready.

The missing piece isn't control over when each team runs. It is a shared record of how the teams' work connects, and what happened when each part last ran.

Weaver takes a different approach. It lets teams keep their own projects and schedules, but still see how their work connects across workspaces. The following experiment shows how.

4. The 50-workspace test

The test used 50 Fabric workspaces on an FTL64 trial capacity in Australia East. Each represented a team with its own project, configuration and Weaver catalogue: a Fabric Warehouse in the team's workspace that records what each of its objects reads and what happened when it last loaded. Each team was initialised, built and loaded separately. The teams and their tables were synthetic and small, so the test measures coordination across workspaces rather than data volume.

Each business team read the central Gold tables through shortcuts in a small bridge Lakehouse in its own workspace. Two business teams read each other's summaries, forming a dependency cycle across workspaces.

To start from a known state, the test first loaded every team once. In practice, each team would run on its own schedule. The test then broke the central Bronze table BRZ.Customer and followed the central team and Team 05 through the failure and recovery.

5.1 The central team's assessment

From one failed Bronze load, the central team traced 61 Warehouse tables, five Lakehouse tables and 11 semantic models in 45 downstream workspaces, without checking job histories one team at a time. It read the business teams' catalogues to do so, but none of their data. An affected object hadn't necessarily failed: it could still hold its last good result.

5.2 Team 05's recovery

Team 05 found the cause from its own Health report, without asking the central team. Once the central team had repaired its objects, Team 05 reloaded just its two tables with one scheduled load --stale command, which reads the two central catalogues for context. It didn't wait for the other business teams, and their work didn't run.

Each side read the other's catalogue, and neither needed access to the other's data. The across-catalogue guide covers the setup and permissions.

6. Repeating the test at 500 workspaces

The same setup was then repeated at ten times the scale: three central workspaces and 497 business teams, on the same FTL64 trial capacity.

  • A full Health check of all 500 catalogues, reading every catalogue and inventorying its physical targets, takes five to seven minutes from a desktop.
  • Team 05's Health report named the cause in about 13 seconds, against 11 seconds at 50 workspaces.

Team 05 reads only the catalogues it depends on, so its own investigation changed little with ten times as many teams in the estate.

7. Conclusion

Self-service in Fabric rarely follows a central design. Central teams publish trusted data and move on, business teams load on schedules that are good enough on most days, and the connections between them grow one shortcut at a time. A failure in that arrangement raises different questions for the central team and for each business team, and neither a single central scheduler nor 50 separate ones answers them.

Weaver is a lightweight framework that works the way a Fabric organisation already runs. Each team gets a complete data engineering framework for its own project: builds, incremental loads, tests and an operational record in its own workspace. The same catalogue gives every team a common record that others can read, so visibility is decentralised in the same way as ownership. There is no separate platform or hosted runtime to install, and no central scheduler for every team to stay in step with. Weaver runs from a desktop, or from a Fabric notebook after pip install weaverstack.

In the test, the central team traced a failed table to 77 affected objects across 50 workspaces. Team 05 found the same cause in 11 seconds, 13 at 500 workspaces, and reloaded only its two tables. That is how Weaver makes Fabric easier to use, and safer to scale.
