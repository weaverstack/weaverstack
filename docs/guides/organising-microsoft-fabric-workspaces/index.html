Fabric Engineering Guides

Organising Microsoft Fabric workspaces for enterprise data sharing

Microsoft Fabric makes it easy for teams across your organisation to share data. As more teams build on one another's work, you also need to see how that work connects: what a failure affects, and what needs to run once it is fixed. This guide shows one way to organise Fabric workspaces with a separate project for each team, and how to trace and recover from a failure across them. It was tested across 50 workspaces and repeated at 500.

Why organisations share data in Microsoft Fabric

When your organisation chooses Microsoft Fabric, one reason is often its enterprise sharing. OneLake gives the whole tenant one data lake, shortcuts let one team read another team's tables without copying them, and workspaces separate each team's items and permissions. A central data team can publish trusted data once, and business teams can build on it directly instead of maintaining copies.

Sharing on that scale also creates a coordination problem. Once dozens or hundreds of teams read one another's data, a failure in one workspace can affect tables and reports in many others, and no single team's job history shows how far it reached.

1. Shared data across separate workspaces

Consider the following setup, which is common when an organisation adopts Fabric for self-service data:

  • A central team owns the Bronze Lakehouse in one workspace.
  • Another team owns the Silver Lakehouse and Gold Warehouse in a second.
  • Shared semantic models live in a third.
  • Dozens or hundreds of business areas each have a workspace. They read central data, and sometimes one another's.

Now suppose the central customer load fails overnight, and you run Finance's Warehouse. Three questions follow:

  • Which tables and reports across the organisation are affected?
  • Are any of Finance's tables built on the failed data?
  • Once the central team repairs it, what should Finance reload, and when?

A central scheduler can coordinate the loads, or teams can run on separate schedules. Either way, Finance cannot answer these questions from Finance's job history, because the cause sits in another team's workspace.

This guide answers them by connecting two records across teams: what each table reads, and what happened when it last loaded.

2. What Fabric provides

Fabric already provides two of the foundations: every item and object can be named precisely, and OneLake connects data across workspaces.

2.1 Fully qualified object names

When someone says CustomerSales failed, you need to know which team's table they mean. For a Warehouse table, the workspace, item type, item name, schema and object name identify it exactly. A Folder uses a path instead of a schema; a semantic model or Report is an item in its own right.

2.2 OneLake shares data across workspaces

Every Fabric tenant has a single OneLake (Microsoft 2026a). Its shortcuts let an item in one workspace read data held by an item in another, including a Lakehouse reading a Warehouse, without copying it (Microsoft 2026b).

Spark can address tables in other workspaces directly by four-part name. Warehouse T-SQL reaches only items in its own workspace, so each business team in this test brought the central Gold tables into its workspace through shortcuts in a small bridge Lakehouse, and read them through that Lakehouse's SQL analytics endpoint.

Fabric also offers item lineage and impact analysis across workspaces. Recovering from a failure needs one level more: how the tables and loads themselves depend on one another, which loads need attention, and what should run next.

3. Dependency and load records

To answer Finance's questions, you need two records connected across every team:

3.1 Dependencies between objects

Finance's shortcut already names the central item and path it reads. Bring both teams' catalogues together, and Weaver matches that source to the central team's table.

3.2 Load status

A successful Finance load can still be behind if the central customer table has been repaired since it ran. Compare Finance's load record with its source, and you can tell a failed step from work that simply needs catching up. The same comparison traces a central failure through every table and model that depends on it.

You choose which teams' catalogues to include, from a pair of teams to the whole organisation, and Weaver connects their dependencies for that investigation or run.

3.3 Running several teams' work together

The same connections let independent teams' work run side by side. Each reader waits only for the sources it needs, including a SQL endpoint refresh or OneLake publication between engines, while unrelated work carries on. The performance guide explains how that scheduling works.

4. The 50-workspace test

The test used 50 Fabric workspaces on an FTL64 trial capacity in Australia East. Each represented a team with a separate catalogue, project and configuration, initialised, built and loaded separately. The teams and their tables were synthetic and small, so the test measures coordination across workspaces rather than data volume.

After running every team together, the test broke the central Bronze table BRZ.Customer, then followed the organisation through finding the impact, repairing the source and recovering each team's work.

5. Connecting the catalogues with Weaver

With Weaver, Weaver keeps these records in a catalogue: a Fabric Warehouse in each team's workspace. Build records the objects and their dependencies; Load and Test record what ran. When you need the wider view, you bring the relevant catalogues together.

5.1 The Weaver CLI

After the central repair, Team 05 catches up with one command. Run from Team 05's project, the command selects Team 05's work and includes the central catalogues that explain its upstream state:

Reload Team 05's stale work

$ weaver load SPREAD_TEAM_05 --workspace SPREAD_TEAM_05 --stale \
  --catalogue SPREAD_TEAM_05/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_BRONZE/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_CENTRAL/Warehouse/Catalogue

The central catalogues provide context; only Team 05's work runs. Select several teams when you want a coordinated run instead. Weaver orders the work and records each result in its owner's catalogue. The across-catalogue guide covers the setup, selectors and permissions.

5.2 The Catalogue Dashboard

If you would rather explore in Power BI, open the Catalogue Dashboard. Select Team 05 on the Workspaces page, follow the teams that supply it, then use Health and Orchestration to investigate the failed load. Every page narrows to your workspace, item or object.

The snapshot below lets you explore ten of the test's 50 catalogues. Direct Lake reads up to ten; for the full set, deploy the Dashboard in Import mode and refresh it to read new records.

6.1 Impact of the failed load

From one failed Bronze load, the central team could trace 61 Warehouse tables, five Lakehouse tables and 11 semantic models in 45 downstream workspaces. Each business team could narrow the same view to its workspace and see the failed source named as the cause, without checking job histories one team at a time. An affected object had not necessarily failed: it could still hold its last good result.

The four thin-report workspaces read the central semantic model, so their reports recover when that model does.

6.2 Recovery by each team

The central team repaired and reloaded the central objects first. Team 05 then saw that its upstream data was fixed and reloaded just its two tables, without waiting for the other business teams. The central catalogues told Team 05 what was ready; they did not pull other teams' work into its run.

6.3 Teams that read one another's data

Teams 01 and 02 read each other's summaries. Weaver ran the cycle once, in a consistent order, and reported it in Health; the one amber finding in the final check was on that cycle. The cycle guide explains how cycles run and how Health assesses them.

6.4 Coordinated recovery

When a wider recovery is needed, select the teams together and add --stale: Weaver loads only the work whose recorded state needs it, in dependency order. In this test, one run brought the remaining stale work in 43 workspaces up to date in just over four minutes.

7. Repeating the test at 500 workspaces

The same setup was then repeated at ten times the scale: three central workspaces and 497 business teams, with 299 Marts, 100 Models, 49 Lakehouses and 49 thin reports, on the same FTL64 trial capacity.

  • A coordinated load of every team ran 755 loads and 405 readiness steps across all 500 catalogues. All 1,160 steps succeeded under one workflow ID, in about 39 minutes.
  • After the same Bronze failure, Health traced 752 downstream tables and models in 450 workspaces: 601 Warehouse tables, 50 Lakehouse tables and 101 semantic models.
  • Team 05's Health report named the cause in about 13 seconds. Team 05 then checked and reloaded its two tables in about 5 and 20 seconds, against 5 and 15 seconds at 50 workspaces.
  • A full Health check of all 500 catalogues, reading every catalogue and inventorying its physical targets, takes five to seven minutes from a desktop.

8. Conclusion

Coordinating work across Fabric workspaces is a common challenge once teams start sharing data. Teams work in separate projects on separate schedules, but a failure in one workspace can reach tables and reports in many others, and no single team's job history shows how far.

Weaver helps by connecting two records across those projects: what each table reads, and what happened when it last loaded. With both connected, you can trace a failure to everything it affects, and reload only the work that needs it.

In the test, the failed central table was traced to 77 affected objects across 50 workspaces and 752 across 500. Team 05 used the same records to find its two stale tables and reload only those.
