Fabric Engineering Guides

Organising Fabric workspaces for independent teams

Give your teams their own Fabric projects and schedules, and you still need a way to follow the data between them. This experiment traced a shared-source failure across 500 real Fabric workspaces, tested a team's local recovery and recorded where the wider campaign failed.

Introduction

When you are deciding how to organise Microsoft Fabric workspaces, you are also deciding who owns the data, who can change it and how teams will depend on one another. A workspace per team gives you a clear place for that ownership. The next question is how to keep their work connected as they start sharing data.

Suppose you run Finance's Warehouse. Your team owns its tables and its schedule, but your sales analysis reads a customer table maintained centrally. That customer load fails. Which of your tables are affected? Once the central team repairs it, what should you reload?

You need answers at both levels: the central team needs to see the reach of the failure, and you need to recover Finance's work on your own schedule. Keeping separate projects should make ownership clearer, not leave you reconstructing the connections during an incident.

1. Self-service data and the coordination problem

If you are organising Fabric for self-service data, this arrangement may look familiar:

  • A central team owns the Bronze Lakehouse in one workspace.
  • Another team owns the Silver Lakehouse and Gold Warehouse in a second.
  • Shared semantic models live in a third.
  • Hundreds of business areas own workspaces of their own. They read central data and occasionally one another's.

A central scheduler can coordinate those loads; separate schedules let teams choose when to run. Either way, Finance still needs to know whether its last successful load used the latest central data. Looking only at Finance's job history will not answer that question.

Keep the teams' ownership, then connect the dependency and load records they already need. That gives you a way to investigate one team's work or coordinate a wider recovery, without making every change go through a shared project.

2. What Fabric already provides

You can build on two things Fabric already gives you: identifiable items and objects, and OneLake connections between workspaces. The data can stay where its team owns it.

2.1 Qualify the object you mean

When someone says CustomerSales failed, you need to know which team's table they mean. For this Warehouse table, the workspace, item type, item name, schema and object name give you that context. A Folder uses a path instead of a schema; a model or Report is an item in its own right.

Finance's shortcut already names the central source it reads. Give Weaver the participating catalogues and it matches that source to the central team's recorded installation. You can follow the connection without maintaining another cross-team map.

2.2 OneLake shares data across workspaces

Every Fabric tenant comes with a single OneLake instance (Microsoft 2026a). Its shortcuts let an item in one workspace read data held by an item in another, including a Lakehouse reading a Warehouse, without copying it (Microsoft 2026b).

In this experiment, Spark addressed tables in other workspaces directly by four-part name. A Warehouse's T-SQL reached only items in its own workspace, so each business team brought central Gold tables into its workspace through shortcuts in a small bridge Lakehouse and read them through that Lakehouse's SQL analytics endpoint.

Fabric also supplies item lineage and impact analysis, including downstream items in other workspaces. The question here is more specific: how do the managed tables and loads depend on one another, which recorded operations need attention, and what should run next?

3. The two missing pieces

To answer Finance's recovery question, you need to connect two records: what each table reads, and what happened when it last loaded.

3.1 Connect the recorded dependencies

Finance's shortcut already identifies the central item and path it reads. Given both catalogues, Weaver matches that source to the central team's installed table and connects the recorded dependencies. The teams do not need to maintain the same connection again in a second map.

3.2 Follow the load results

A successful Finance load can still be behind: the central customer table may have been repaired since it ran. Compare Finance's load record with the source's state and you can distinguish a failed step from work that simply needs catching up. You can also trace the central failure through the tables and models that depend on it.

Each team keeps its own catalogue. Choose the catalogues to include, and Weaver connects their recorded dependencies for the investigation or run. You can inspect a pair of teams or bring the participating catalogues together for a wider view.

3.3 A bonus: concurrency

Those same connections let you run independent teams' work together. Each reader waits for the source it needs, including a SQL endpoint refresh or OneLake publication between engines; unrelated branches can continue. The performance guide explains how that scheduling works within a project.

4. Experimental setup

The experiment used 500 real Fabric workspaces on an FTL64 trial capacity in Australia East. Each represented an independently owned project with its own catalogue and configuration. The teams and small tables were synthetic; the experiment tested dependency tracking and execution across workspaces, not data-volume throughput.

5. The implementation: Weaver

With Weaver, each team keeps those records in its own Fabric Warehouse catalogue. Build records the objects and dependencies; Load and Test record what ran. To investigate connected work, you bring the relevant catalogues together. The teams keep their projects where they are.

5.1 The Weaver CLI

For Team 05, catching up after the central repair is one command. From its configured project, name its own work and include the central catalogues that explain its upstream state:

Reload Team 05's stale work

$ weaver load SPREAD_TEAM_05 --workspace SPREAD_TEAM_05 --stale \
  --catalogue SPREAD_TEAM_05/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_BRONZE/Warehouse/Catalogue \
  --catalogue SPREAD_CENTRAL/Warehouse/Catalogue

The central catalogues provide context; only Team 05's work is selected to run. Select several teams when you want a coordinated run instead. Weaver orders that selection and records each result in its owner's catalogue. The across-catalogue guide covers the setup, selectors and required permissions.

5.2 The Catalogue Dashboard

If you would rather explore the connections in Power BI, open the Catalogue Dashboard. Select Team 05 in Workspaces, follow the teams that supply it, then use Health and Orchestration to investigate the failed load. You can narrow the same pages to your workspace, item or object.

The earlier snapshot below illustrates the interface over ten catalogues; it is not the 500-workspace result. For the full set, deploy the Dashboard in Import mode and refresh it to read new records; Direct Lake supports up to ten catalogues.

6.1 Knowing the impact of a failure

The impact assessment traced the failed Bronze load to 601 Warehouse tables, 50 Lakehouse tables and 101 semantic models in 450 downstream workspaces. That count excludes the failed source itself and counts each managed object once, even when it had several findings. An affected object was not necessarily failed: it could still hold an earlier successful result.

The 49 thin-report workspaces read the central semantic model. Their data recovery follows that model; they are not counted as additional managed table or model loads.

6.2 Each team recovers on its own terms

After building the repair, the central team selected its own work. Six object loads and four readiness steps completed across the three central catalogues. Team 05 then checked its two Mart tables, saw that their upstream data had been repaired, and loaded its own stale work. Its two object loads succeeded, and its subsequent catalogue-only Health report was green.

The upstream catalogues supplied context and readiness requirements. They did not expand Team 05's object-load selection to every other team. The remaining stale recovery was a separate operation with its own result.

6.3 Teams that read one another's data

Teams 01 and 02 read each other's summaries. The cycle appeared in both the baseline and post-recovery Health reports. The post-recovery check left one amber stale-ancestor finding on that cycle rather than claiming every catalogue was green. The cycle guide explains the execution and assessment rules.

6.4 Coordination and its recorded failure

The initial all-catalogue baseline and the later selected stale recovery succeeded. A further full coordinated Load did not: Team 496's semantic-model refresh failed after 1,159 other steps succeeded. The campaign therefore lacks a successful final coordinated Load and its post-Load Health check.

A subsequent managed candidate run also stopped during its impact assessment, with 388 unreadable catalogues, before reaching recovery. Neither attempt is a qualified end-to-end 500-workspace campaign. Successful individual stages and the read-only checks below do not remove those failures.

6.5 Faster Health, measured separately

A later unreleased desktop candidate checked the same 500-workspace estate with every default target inventory enabled. Two fresh-client checks took about seven minutes and five minutes, including Session close and isolated native-driver cleanup. The first missed the six-minute target; the second passed it. These are observed timings, not a guarantee that every check will finish within six minutes.

These checks included discovery, catalogue reads, physical target inventories and joined assessment. Complete means every selected catalogue was readable and assessed, not that every catalogue was green. Their later operational state differs from the earlier fault-and-recovery exercise. These are Health measurements, not new Load or recovery results.

7. Team ownership, coordinated operation

You can give teams control of their Fabric work without losing the ability to operate across it. Keep each team's project, catalogue and schedule; use the recorded connections when a shared failure or coordinated run needs a wider view.

  1. When you own a shared source:See which teams depend on it and coordinate the selected recovery.
  2. When your team consumes that source:Understand what held up your work and catch it up when you are ready.

The useful result is that choice. The same records support a team's local decision and an organisation-wide run. You can coordinate when it helps, without turning independent teams into one shared deployment.
